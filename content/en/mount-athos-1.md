When traveling in Greece, most people go to Athens, Santorini, or Crete. But in northern Greece, there is another very special place hidden away: Mount Athos. It is not a tourist attraction in the usual sense, nor a place where you can simply buy a ticket and walk in. It is a peninsula governed by Eastern Orthodox monks, still operating under an order that feels almost a thousand years old.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/86F1CAA1-4F24-48A0-BA2C-6A18B8266842_1_105_c.jpeg)

I first heard about this place roughly a year ago, and since then I had always been curious about it: what would a place be like that sits inside the European Union, inside modern Greece, yet seems to have remained somewhere in the Middle Ages?

“Here is the end of the human world, the final outlet of desire, the frontier of reality” — this is how Haruki Murakami described this “holy land” in *Rainy Days, Burning Sun*, after traveling to Mount Athos in the 1980s. On this trip to Europe, I decided to set aside four days and three nights to see it for myself.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260603162536.png)

Mount Athos is located at the easternmost tip of the Halkidiki peninsula in northern Greece. It is one of the holiest and most unusual corners of the Eastern Orthodox world. Across the peninsula are twenty monasteries, still governed by monks today, forming a semi-autonomous “Monastic Republic of Mount Athos” — sovereign territory of Greece, but with its own religious, administrative, and everyday order.

And perhaps the most surprising rule is this: Mount Athos is still open only to men. Women are not allowed to enter, and according to tradition, even many female animals are excluded. To a modern visitor, this rule feels almost unbelievable. Yet it is also precisely this isolation that has preserved here an exceptionally rare medieval monastic atmosphere — no hotels, no shopping streets, no conventional tourism development. The rhythm of the whole peninsula still revolves around monasteries, prayer, hiking, and sea transport.

![In areas where vehicles cannot easily pass, supplies are still transported by horses](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/B1BD7E5A-5A3E-433A-B488-DE1EA7F47DC0_1_105_c.jpeg)

## Applying for Entry

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260605151526.png)

Mount Athos is not a place you can simply decide to visit on a whim. The number of visitors allowed to enter each day is strictly limited. For individual travelers, the general quota is 10 non-Orthodox visitors and 100 Orthodox visitors per day. The remaining visitors usually come through invitations, religious visits, or organized groups. In other words, for an ordinary traveler like me, the first problem was not transportation or accommodation, but whether I could get permission to enter at all.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260605151016.png)

I started the application process about a month and a half in advance. Mount Athos has an officially maintained [website](https://mount-athos.org/en), which contains information on permits, transportation, and the monasteries. I submitted my personal information, passport details, and intended dates of entry according to the instructions, and later received an email confirmation of my entry permission. The actual paper permit, however, has to be collected in person on the day of departure at the office in Ouranoupoli.

After securing permission to enter, I still had to contact the monasteries on the mountain myself to arrange accommodation. Mount Athos has 20 main monasteries, 17 of which belong to the Greek Orthodox tradition, while the other three belong respectively to the Serbian, Bulgarian, and Russian traditions. In addition, there are 12 smaller monastic communities called sketes, which sit somewhere between large monasteries and solitary hermitages, scattered across the peninsula.

![The first line of the email was “Dear Fathers” — addressing the priests, kind of funny hhh](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260603163811.png)

This step turned out to be much more troublesome than I had expected. Ordinary visitors can stay for a maximum of four days and three nights, and each monastery usually only allows a visitor to stay for one night. So arranging accommodation is not as simple as randomly choosing three monasteries. You also have to think carefully about the route for each day. There is no ordinary public transport network on Mount Athos. Many places can only be reached on foot, by boat, or by a small number of minibuses. If you stay somewhere too remote on one night, you may have to walk for hours the next day, or even miss the boat.

So while arranging accommodation, I was constantly checking the map, looking up ferry schedules, and emailing different monasteries. Once the permit and accommodations were more or less settled, I finally confirmed my plan to visit Mount Athos from May 25 to May 28.

## Transfer in Thessaloniki

The most common route to Mount Athos is to first arrive in Thessaloniki, then take a bus to Ouranoupoli, and finally board a boat to Mount Athos.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/x.png)

Thessaloniki is a relatively overlooked city in Greek travel. When most people think of Greece, they first think of Athens, the Acropolis, and ancient Greek civilization. But if we shift the perspective beyond the classical era, Thessaloniki is actually an extremely important place. For a long time, it was one of the most important cities of the Eastern Roman Empire after Constantinople, as well as a commercial and religious center of the Balkans. In a sense, if Athens represents the “ancient Greece” people imagine, then Thessaloniki represents another side of Greece: Orthodox, Byzantine, and Balkan.

Modern Greece is often understood from the outside as the heir to ancient Greece. But in reality, the language, religion, and many cultural traditions of Greece today are also deeply rooted in the Eastern Roman / Byzantine world.

![Roman ruins can be seen throughout the city](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/E5490ECE-D960-4C2C-B333-F33D2BF9FAEE_1_105_c.jpeg)

The city’s history is also extremely complex. During the Ottoman period, Thessaloniki had one of the most important Jewish communities in Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean world. In modern times, however, the great fire of 1917 almost reshaped the entire city center. It then went through the Balkan Wars, the Greek-Turkish population exchange, the redrawing of national borders, and the systematic murder of its Jewish population by the Nazis during the Second World War. Many of the ethnic groups, languages, and communities that once made up the everyday life of this city later disappeared in the violent turns of history.

![A very pleasant seaside city](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/31BF825E-B731-4678-B715-E3D6FEA3F1B5_1_105_c.jpeg)

Perhaps because of this, Thessaloniki is not as easy to present as a tourist city as Athens. Athens has a very clear narrative: ancient Greece, democracy, philosophy, the Acropolis. Thessaloniki’s layers are messier: Roman and Byzantine, Ottoman and Jewish, Balkan nationalism and the modern Greek state. I really like cities with this kind of complexity.

![Overlooking the city from the old town on the hill](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/81EB0873-2264-42CA-A859-7214F294BDE1_1_105_c.jpeg)

On the taxi ride from the airport into the city, I chatted with the driver about my plan to go to Mount Athos. He said he had gone there last year as well. Later, after looking it up, I realized that a very large number of Greeks actually visit Mount Athos every year — roughly over a hundred thousand visits annually — and Greek locals are the main group of visitors.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/DDB0A674-BF50-4DC1-9A6C-FD67EE66B1EB_1_105_c.jpeg)

## Ouranoupoli: The Night Before Entering Mount Athos

After spending a short day in Thessaloniki, I took a bus the next afternoon to Ouranoupoli — the most common gateway town for people heading to Mount Athos.

The bus drove eastward, gradually leaving the edges of Thessaloniki behind. The scenery outside the window changed little by little: first ordinary suburban roads, then hills, grass slopes, and scattered villages, and finally the view suddenly opened up, with the Aegean Sea appearing in the distance. The whole journey felt as if I was slowly leaving the modern world and heading toward something older. After more than two bumpy hours, the bus finally arrived in Ouranoupoli.

It was around four in the afternoon when I arrived, but the streets were almost empty. Later I realized that the rhythm of this small town is closely tied to the boat schedule for Mount Athos. The liveliest times of day are basically when boats arrive and depart. Pilgrims, visitors, monks, and supplies all appear around those moments; at other times, the whole town quickly falls quiet again.

The guesthouse I stayed at was run by an elderly grandmother who did not speak English. She took me upstairs and introduced the room, the keys, hot water, and various facilities entirely in Greek. I understood none of it, so I could only keep nodding along, trying my best to look like someone who had understood everything perfectly.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260605103024.png)

Ouranoupoli itself is not large. Along the main street, there are many shops selling items related to Mount Athos and Eastern Orthodoxy: icons, crosses, prayer ropes, candles, incense, as well as honey, olive oil, and wine produced by the monasteries.

On one hand, this is just an ordinary seaside town. On the other hand, it is also the buffer zone before entering Mount Athos. One step further, and you enter a world where women are forbidden, monks govern themselves, and life follows a completely different order. But here, you can still buy Coke, book a hotel, and eat at a restaurant.

![There are many Mount Athos / Eastern Orthodox gift shops on the street](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260605103403.png)

![Since I was not sure whether food would be convenient after entering the mountain, I bought some snacks at the supermarket to carry with me. Later I found that this was actually quite necessary...](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260605103850.png)

After dinner, I returned to the hotel and checked the next day’s itinerary one last time. Once everything was ready, I went to bed early. The next day, I would finally enter Mount Athos.
