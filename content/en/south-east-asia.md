Starting in February 2026, I traveled through ten cities in sequence: Manila in the Philippines; Jakarta and Yogyakarta in Indonesia; Bangkok in Thailand; Phnom Penh in Cambodia; Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi in Vietnam; Hong Kong; Macau; and Bandar Seri Begawan in Brunei.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260316162316.png)

Compared with the relatively unified cultural thread I felt in Latin America [(notes on Latin America)](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/BZRlNXWz79DpIdpbBLioZQ), Southeast Asian countries differ enormously from one another, both historically and today. Every time I changed cities, I felt I was entering a completely new world. Add in the current global landscape, with people joking that the Americas belong to the U.S. and Asia belongs to China, and it was also interesting to observe the many visible and invisible links each place has with China.

---

## Cities and cities

At the end of January I left Vancouver and, after a 13-hour transoceanic flight, arrived in Manila. The Philippines is the only predominantly Catholic country in Southeast Asia. It was colonized by Spain and later the United States, and Manila was almost flattened by repeated Japanese bombing during the Pacific War. The weight of history there felt heavier than I had expected. I will also be staying in Manila for a while in the coming months, so I imagine I will see it in a different light then.

![Manila has the oldest Chinatown in the world, dating back to the 16th century](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/E10A4D52-040B-4E92-8168-D880D50F0EAD_1_105_c.jpeg)

Then I crossed the equator into the Southern Hemisphere and reached Jakarta and Yogyakarta in Indonesia, one representing modern Indonesia and the other ancient Indonesia. Jakarta is full of skyscrapers and traffic, but one turn at a street corner can drop you into chaos that feels almost like a slum. Yogyakarta, by contrast, preserves the essence of Javanese culture. The ruins of Borobudur and Prambanan are so grand that you end up standing there much longer than planned, struck by the deep marks Buddhism and Hinduism left on this land.    
With more than three hundred islands, hundreds of languages and ethnic groups, and the status of the world’s largest Muslim-majority country, Indonesia still feels remarkably open and tolerant. The fact that it managed to cohere into a single national form at all is incredible. Overall, the country gave me a strong sense of vitality.

![The old Dutch East India Company site in central Jakarta. At its peak, the company was worth more than Nvidia, Microsoft, and Google combined today. Truly the biggest “tech giant” in history](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260316150509.png)

After flying to Bangkok, the biggest surprise was how many white people there were. The racial mix honestly felt comparable to cities on the east coast of North America. Glittering Buddhist temples, portraits of the monarchy everywhere, shopping streets that rival Shibuya, the smell of weed drifting through entire blocks, Khao San Road being called backpacker heaven... Bangkok has more range and density than even New York in my mind. It seems able to accommodate almost any lifestyle, which probably explains why digital nomads love it so much. I also spent a few days there living like a fully useless pleasure-seeking ruler.

![Bangkok’s Chinatown felt exactly like the Hong Kong-gangster-movie stereotype, just packed with people](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260316151742.png)

Then came Phnom Penh. I chose Cambodia purely because of Pol Pot. His name alone was enough of a reason, to the point that I directly gave up Angkor Wat in Siem Reap. Phnom Penh is a "naked" city: dense Indian, Pakistani, and Bangladeshi restaurants, streets that feel almost like a semi-red-light district, and new development on the outskirts that looked oddly similar to Beijing suburbia.

The main stop was the S-21 genocide museum. Standing there, I felt very clearly that compared with simple corruption and lust for power, totalitarians driven by ideology are much more dangerous. The trauma of history still hangs over the place.

![The Choeung Ek killing field. The entire tower is filled with skulls of people murdered by the Khmer Rouge, and this is only part of them](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/27D7D713-CE1B-4FC7-8503-D2609150FC21_1_105_c.jpeg)

Vietnam was the country I was most interested in on this trip. Watching a branch of Chinese civilization that split off a thousand years ago, at once familiar and distant, was just too interesting. Saigon’s historical changes and urban layout feel strikingly similar to Shanghai, while Hanoi functions more like Beijing, though in terms of openness and diversity it loses nothing to the south.   

The streets are full of portraits of Ho Chi Minh and hammer-and-sickle flags, but at its core Vietnam is actually quite pragmatic. Politically it has a kind of power-balancing arrangement among four major offices, and personality cult is largely limited to Ho Chi Minh as a source of historical legitimacy rather than spreading everywhere. Both cities also felt very diverse.

At the war museum, the exhibits on French and American invasion left a strong impression on me. A people that has repeatedly been brutalized by stronger neighbors and still survived must have a certain resilience. Today the country also feels energetic and upward-moving, a bit like reform-era China.

![The Independence Palace in Ho Chi Minh City, once the seat of South Vietnam. Jokes aside, compared with other “suns,” Ho Chi Minh probably deserves the title of “great leader” the most](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/D9DAB6CA-433F-4517-8FBF-287986DF9FA7_1_105_c.jpeg)

My Hong Kong and Macau stop happened to overlap with Lunar New Year, so many sights were closed, and by the end of the trip I was also a little tired, so I did not go very deep. In my mind these two places had always been mentioned together, but in reality the atmosphere is quite different. Hong Kong has the tension of a metropolis; Macau has the laziness of a small town. Each has its own base note. My original plan was to take a helicopter from Hong Kong to Macau, but that day happened to fall on one of the days of the week with no flights, so that idea died.

![I happened to catch Lunar New Year celebrations in Hong Kong](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/B21D5EC8-8B86-4813-9B9B-AC4EC79A494F_1_105_c.jpeg)

Last was Brunei, the most obscure and also the strangest country I have visited so far. The Malay world, Arab culture, Commonwealth traditions, and the Chinese community all overlap there, and once you add oil wealth, an absolute monarchy, and strict Islamic law, everything feels stitched together in a way that is hard to describe. Unfortunately I arrived during Ramadan, so the mosques were all closed, and the entire city felt unusually quiet and empty.

![The taxi driver I met in Brunei even had WeChat to attract Chinese customers. That was unexpected](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/E2DE125B-CC25-47C2-82E8-A1F73A694FB8_1_105_c.jpeg)

## People and people


Along the way I also met many interesting people.

In the Philippines, I hired a guide in Intramuros. At the end of the tour we happened to be walking along the Pasig River, and I casually asked whether Spanish or Japanese visitors ever came there to look for history and apologize. He said yes, they did: "but they should apologize." His tone was calm, not angry, just stating something he saw as obvious.

At the history museum in Jakarta, I happened to meet an elderly ethnic Chinese man and talked with him for a long time about stories of Chinese Indonesians that are not well known. Before we parted, he left me his WhatsApp and said that if I had any questions about Southeast Asia, I could reach out.

On a day tour in Yogyakarta, I met a 59-year-old man from Seoul who looked more like 39. He had a PhD in tourism management from France, spoke four languages, and had visited more than sixty countries after retirement. What surprised me most was how familiar he was with third- and fourth-tier Chinese cities. It turned out he had been a university professor and had taught many Chinese students, so of course he knew their hometowns well. We talked a lot about the situation of young people in Korea. He mentioned how hot semiconductors are and said military service is not nearly as terrifying as outsiders often imagine.

One night, while walking by the river in Phnom Penh, I was stopped by a heavily made-up Lao woman who wanted to chat. She said many young women there make a living by approaching white men, and there was a bit of helplessness in her tone. But then she started asking where I was staying, invited me to sit and look at the river, and kept leaning toward me with her hands and body. ~~As a man of integrity, naturally~~ I began to feel the situation turning sketchy, so after a few polite lines I found an excuse to leave. Quite an encounter.

In Hanoi, I ran into trouble when visiting the Ho Chi Minh Museum because the ticket office only accepted cash, and my cash was stored far away. I asked a nearby shop whether I could use a card and exchange for cash. The family simply handed me 50,000 dong and wished me a happy new year. That unprompted kindness surprised me a lot. By the time I got the money and rushed back to the museum, it had already closed for lunch, so I returned the cash to them.

In a hostel in Hong Kong, I met a French guy who felt like a walking Paris stereotype: stylish leather jacket, black jeans, rolling a large suitcase. He turned out to be an old-school backpacker. Whenever he ran out of money, he would go back to Paris, work for a while, save up, and head out again. He had spent years doing working holidays in Australia and New Zealand. He had just flown in from Paris and was heading next to Shenzhen, Shanghai, Beijing... very free in the best sense.

## A few thoughts

The stereotype of Southeast Asia is usually islands, cheap prices, and a laid-back paradise. But after traveling through it, I feel the region is seriously underrated. Historically, this land gave birth to highly complex civilizations in its own right: Champa, Angkor, Siam... Its gradual rise today is not accidental. It feels more like a return to historical position, and in some ways faintly resembles China over the past few decades.

Colonialism and suffering left scars, but not too much baggage, so society ended up quite diverse; unpredictable natural disasters and tropical culture also seem to give people a relatively relaxed temperament; and with the execution power that comes from East Asian-style authoritarian order, many countries have already entered a phase of fast growth. Corruption and inequality, of course, remain unavoidable costs, but that seems to be a stage almost every rapidly developing society passes through.

From a personal point of view, cities like Bangkok, Ho Chi Minh City, and Hanoi are all quite attractive options for long stays or remote-work geographic arbitrage. ~~(If I ever get laid off, spending a year or two here honestly sounds pretty good.)~~ The downside is that traffic congestion is almost universal, and air quality and food safety are not very reassuring either. Compared with North America or China’s first-tier cities, I do not think this is yet the right place for long-term settlement.
