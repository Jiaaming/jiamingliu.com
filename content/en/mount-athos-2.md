At seven the next morning, I got up, washed, packed my things, returned the key to my host, and prepared to finally enter Mount Athos.

![Day 1 route](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260912223410.png)

The route for the day looked rather complicated: first, I would take a ferry down the peninsula’s western coast to the port of Dafni; from there, a bus would take me inland to Karyes, the capital; then I would transfer to a minibus for Vatopedi Monastery on the northeastern coast. On the map, I was merely moving from one side of the peninsula to the other. In practice, it meant changing between a ferry, a bus, and a minibus.

## Entering Mount Athos

The Pilgrims’ Office was only about a hundred meters from my guesthouse. The street, so quiet the previous afternoon, was now crowded with men preparing to enter the mountain. Most had come in groups and carried bulging backpacks; some also had trekking poles. Unlike me, a visitor mostly interested in looking around, many of them were clearly prepared to cover a serious distance on foot.

![The Pilgrims’ Office the previous afternoon; by the next morning, a line had formed outside](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260703102526.png)

My ferry was scheduled to leave at 9:45, but to be safe, I arrived two hours early. The office had four or five windows open, so the line moved quickly. When my turn came, the staff member checked my confirmation email and passport, then asked about my religion. I paused for a moment before deciding to answer honestly: “No religion.”

After paying thirty euros, I received my official entry permit—the Diamonitirion. For the next few days, I would need this sheet of paper to board boats, take buses, and stay at the monasteries. Only when I finally held it in my hands did I feel, for the first time, that there was no turning back.

![My Diamonitirion, the entry permit for Mount Athos](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/A14A7C97-6B2E-464F-A343-BC7EDDAB8073_1_105_c.jpeg)

With the permit collected, I followed the crowd to the ticket booth by the harbor and bought my ferry ticket to Dafni.

![Ferry ticket to the port of Dafni](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/B6C2F929-81E7-46EF-A449-C3102BE2A9F8_1_105_c.jpeg)

There was still more than an hour before departure—enough time for one “last secular breakfast.” All the shops beside the ticket booth were open, and the street was full of pilgrims waiting to board. At a nearby restaurant, faced with a choice between an American breakfast and a Greek breakfast, I decisively chose the latter in the spirit of cultural immersion. It cost about thirteen euros. Unfortunately, it was far more austere than I had imagined, and the coffee was astonishingly bitter. I had barely started eating before regretting my choice... Still, since I would have no control over what I ate once I entered the mountain, I obediently finished everything.

![A Greek breakfast costing around thirteen euros: eggs, cheese, cucumber, bread, and a very bitter cup of Greek coffee](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/8603EEAE-E5D0-4064-8174-4B41365CA0B2_1_105_c.jpeg)

## Leaving the Secular World Behind

Boarding opened at around 9:20. The staff simply scanned each ticket and let us through, making the process much easier than I had expected. I boarded early and went straight to the upper deck, where I found a seat overlooking the sea.

![The ferry at Ouranoupoli, preparing to depart for Dafni](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/7F39E6F7-613F-4237-9656-A412F9F30B67_1_105_c.jpeg)

At 9:45, the ferry left on time. The Byzantine tower of Ouranoupoli slowly shrank behind us until it merged into the coastline. The town was still physically close, yet I felt a strange boundary opening up between us. From that moment on, it seemed as though I were withdrawing from familiar secular life and sailing gradually into a different order.

![Looking back at Ouranoupoli from the ferry as the tower and beach receded behind us](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260802144742.png)

Before long, flocks of seagulls caught up with the boat. Some passengers tore leftover bread into pieces and threw them into the air. The birds circled close to the deck, occasionally diving down to snatch a piece. The deep-blue sea, the retreating shore, and an entire boat of silent pilgrims gave this otherwise ordinary scene something of the atmosphere of a religious ritual.

![Pilgrims throwing bread into the air as seagulls followed the ferry](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/2814F0E9-E49E-403F-98AF-D6E691DA5CF7_1_105_c.jpeg)

I looked around and saw almost no other Asian faces. Many passengers appeared to be traveling in groups from the Balkans and Eastern Europe, while a few fathers had brought their sons. Others wore matching black robes and had thick, sometimes graying beards—clearly monks traveling between the Holy Mountain and the outside world. People who knew one another talked and embraced; some bent down to kiss the hands of members of the clergy. Everything looked remarkably like a scene from a religious film.

![Pilgrims on the ferry’s upper deck; most simply sat in silence and watched the sea](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/01310CCD-9E9B-47AE-A5AF-C7024706E8A0_1_105_c.jpeg)

I later learned that Mount Athos’s religious order is written directly into modern Greek law. [Article 105 of the Greek Constitution](https://www.hellenicparliament.gr/vouli-ton-ellinon/to-politevma/syntagma/article-109/) states that anyone admitted there as a novice or monk acquires Greek citizenship—a new immigration strategy, perhaps, hahaha. It felt rather strange to think that the black-robed figures on the ferry might simultaneously belong to a monastic community with a thousand years of history and to a modern nation-state.

![The ferry stopped at several monastic ports along the way, where passengers came and went, although most were bound for Dafni](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/4CAD8D1E-A592-4C9A-8032-B417BF9FC126_1_105_c.jpeg)

The ferry continued south along the western coast. Most of the hillsides were covered in forest, with monastic docks, towers, and churches occasionally appearing along the shore before quickly falling behind us. My phone signal gradually disappeared as well. At first, I kept opening Google Maps to check our position; eventually, I put my phone away and simply watched the view outside.

![Passing the harbor of Zograf Monastery; the monastery itself lies deeper in the valley](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/F4EB5F4E-6E7C-4B17-81AF-93CD0BEA0D1C_1_105_c.jpeg)

## Changing Buses in Karyes

![Pilgrims lining up to disembark](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/9E908328-5D06-4F16-9E96-77ED3D00193B_1_105_c.jpeg)

After about two and a half hours at sea, we finally reached our destination: the port of Dafni. Once the ferry had docked, the crew unloaded the vehicles and supplies before allowing the passengers off. The harbor was small, yet people and cargo were crowded together, making it look like the entire peninsula’s only logistics hub. I followed the crowd to the bus for Karyes and discovered that it accepted card payments. I had spent the previous evening worrying that everything on the mountain would require cash—and had even withdrawn an extra hundred euros—so all that anxiety turned out to have been unnecessary.

![The port of Dafni](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260912223256.png)

The bus wound uphill for a little over forty minutes before reaching Karyes. This is the administrative center of Mount Athos and one of the few places on the peninsula that feels anything like a small town. As soon as I got off, I began looking for the minibus to Vatopedi. Few people nearby spoke much English, so I first tried gesturing and then typed my question into a translation app. Fortunately, the station was not large, and after wandering around for a while, I found the driver with help from a few people nearby.

Before boarding, the staff checked my entry permit again. The minibus held about twelve people, with all our luggage crammed into the rear compartment. The vehicle looked as though it had already spent many years in service. Closing the back door sent up a cloud of dust, and the bags were packed tightly against one another.

![The nearly full minibus from Karyes to Vatopedi](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/A6D38A03-0DE1-4B3B-9AF3-EE170398A9EB_1_105_c.jpeg)

The other passengers soon noticed the unusual Asian face in their midst. When they learned that I came from China, someone made a point of telling me that China is pronounced “Kina” in Greek. I later came across a set of visitor statistics: about 158,000 people entered Mount Athos in 2024, but [only 28 of them came from China](https://en.protothema.gr/2025/07/07/from-umberto-eco-to-mel-gibson-famous-visitors-to-mount-athos-to-the-garden-of-the-virgin-mary/). No wonder people kept giving me a second glance.

The minibus drove northeast for another forty minutes or so. We passed through a checkpoint on the way and had to show our permits yet again. The staff asked me several questions in Greek. I understood none of them and could only reply with a mixture of words and gestures, but somehow I was allowed through without incident.

## Vatopedi Monastery

By the time our minibus arrived, someone from the monastery was already waiting at the entrance. Founded in the tenth century, [Vatopedi](https://www.efachagor.gr/en/place/vatopedi-monastery/) ranks second among Mount Athos’s twenty ruling monasteries, and its complex is correspondingly vast. It even has its own [online accommodation application system](https://hospitality.vatopedi.gr/reservations/login/en), making it the most “digital” of the monasteries I had applied to.

We were first led into the archontariki, or guest reception room. The long tables were already surrounded by newly arrived pilgrims, and the monk responsible for receiving guests brought each person water, a small glass of spirits, and some loukoumi. This is the traditional way monasteries on Mount Athos welcome visitors: there is no need for elaborate small talk; first, they let those who have traveled from afar sit down and rest.

![Vatopedi’s guest reception room, where newly arrived pilgrims sat around long tables](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/69CBAB24-27EF-4632-91B8-3FE80E5384E2_1_105_c.jpeg)

![Water and a small glass of spirits for newly arrived guests, with the day’s schedule beside them](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/EBC697B1-E476-4C38-B6EE-518EFD800B93_1_105_c.jpeg)

The monk first explained the accommodation and service schedule to everyone else in Greek, then repeated it separately for me in English. His English was fluent, and he patiently told me when meals were served, where I could get water, and when the guided tours would begin. I also asked whether I was the first Chinese visitor they had received. He laughed and said of course not—other Chinese visitors had come before me.

The monk then pulled me over to show me the software they used to assign rooms. It displayed more than 160 beds, over a hundred of which were already occupied that day. I was placed in a shared dormitory with several visitors from Romania.

![My dormitory, where the beds, blankets, and towels had all been arranged neatly; the conditions were much better than I had expected](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/6B8F478E-5111-4D89-B847-1CC56ACCB4FC_1_105_c.jpeg)

After settling in, I wandered around the monastery by myself. Vatopedi did not entirely match my idea of a “medieval monastery.” The reception area had automatic coffee machines; rubbish was sorted according to EU standards; solar panels stood on the hillside; and outside the walls were fields and a fishpond. The coffee machine even offered Greek, Russian, Romanian, and several other “national” varieties. They sounded very international, though they all tasted more or less the same to me.

![The coffee machine in the reception area offered several different “national” varieties](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/88B474B3-2A69-4565-80D9-27D91021523E_1_105_c.jpeg)

There was also a small shop nearby selling crosses, icons, and products made by the monastery—really no different from the gift shop at a tourist attraction. The place felt more like a large, mostly self-sufficient community: the monks prayed, but they also farmed, maintained the buildings, welcomed guests, and dealt with the tangible and mundane details of daily life.

![Crosses and religious ornaments in the shop beside the reception area](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/326E40BF-3F7E-4354-90B7-397929200276_1_105_c.jpeg)

![Vatopedi’s central courtyard, with part of the main church on the right](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/3134523C-CA19-424E-8EB1-E3D57C58D414_1_105_c.jpeg)

![Solar panels beside the fields—somehow reminiscent of the secluded utopia in *The Peach Blossom Spring*](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/A386CD60-F3B2-48AC-A786-B1B2B54F5864_1_105_c.jpeg)

Unfortunately, I misheard the monk’s “5 p.m.” as “5 a.m.” By the time I realized my mistake, I had missed both the communal dinner and the English-language tour. Somewhat embarrassed, I returned to the reception room and explained what had happened. I assumed that I would have to survive on the snacks in my backpack, but the monk asked no further questions and soon had a separate meal prepared for me.

Dinner was simple: rice, olives, bread, and a few side dishes. Since I did not know when I would next be fed, I wrapped a piece of bread in paper and carried it back to the dormitory. Like the snacks I had bought before entering the mountain, having that piece of bread with me was strangely reassuring.

![The dinner the monks prepared separately for me after I missed the communal meal](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/4C5DCF35-251C-459B-B28A-82D561C8EFFF_1_105_c.jpeg)

The English tour was already over, so I followed the final Greek-language group instead. I understood almost nothing and could only watch how everyone responded. Many pilgrims had come specifically to see the icons and relics; whenever the guide pointed to an object, the people around me would fall silent. Vatopedi preserves a vast collection of Byzantine art and religious treasures, including numerous frescoes from the eleventh to thirteenth centuries inside the main church. To me, they resembled artifacts in a museum. To the people around me, however, they were sacred objects that still took an active part in the present. 

![I could not understand a single sentence of the Greek tour...](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/49E419F1-DA24-476E-9EAA-B9B34BF8B8E7_1_105_c.jpeg)

Throughout the visit, I remained cautious. As an outsider with no religious faith, I was never quite sure how I should behave: too much enthusiasm would feel insincere, while too much detachment would border on disrespect. In the end, I simply spoke less, followed everyone else, and remained quiet whenever I did not understand. Perhaps that is also the most appropriate way to observe an unfamiliar world—not rushing to judge it, and first admitting that I do not understand it.

![A monk was feeding the fish beside the pond. When he saw me, he stopped to chat and told me that the fish had originally come from Japan](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/03223269-6372-43DD-8DEC-84416FC010D7_1_105_c.jpeg)

After dinner, I took another walk outside the courtyard and eventually found a bench on a raised terrace within the monastery. I sat there as the sun slowly sank behind the mountain, its last light falling across the old stone walls and rooftops while the colors around me gradually faded. A monk would occasionally pass quietly through the courtyard; otherwise, I could hear almost nothing. I stayed on the bench until the entire monastery had settled into silence.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/E8D8A142-0DD9-4B04-9F19-84E84C25452C_1_105_c.jpeg)

When I returned to the dormitory, I finally met my Romanian roommates. They were far more devout than I had expected and soon began discussing religious questions that left me struggling to keep up—but that is another story.
