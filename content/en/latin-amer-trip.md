From January 3 to January 25, 2026, after finishing my Antarctic expedition, I traveled solo from south to north across six countries: Argentina, Uruguay, Chile, Peru, El Salvador, and Mexico. In a single month, I basically lived through all four seasons. It was the longest and most densely packed trip I have taken so far.

Along the way I passed through twelve very different cities: Ushuaia, Buenos Aires, Colonia, Montevideo, Valparaiso, Santiago, Lima, Santa Ana, San Salvador, Puebla, Teotihuacan, and Mexico City.

Because I barely speak any Spanish, I got through almost the entire trip with just “Hola,” “Gracias,” and a translation app. Interacting with strangers in bus terminals, on the street, and in markets turned into a series of little adventures, which was honestly pretty fun. ~~I feel like I’m close to level ten in sign language by now~~

## Comparative politics

Comparing political temperament, historical memory, and official narratives across different countries is one of the things I enjoy most when I travel. Take the Southern Cone as an example. Argentina, Uruguay, and Chile share fairly similar cultural backgrounds and historical starting points, and yet you can still clearly feel that they split into three different paths.

Argentina still carries the air of a country that was once developed. Buenos Aires remains elegant, orderly, and beautiful, but it is also full of performance, fracture, and inner contradiction. On the one hand it still looks toward Europe with deep longing; on the other hand it is constantly pushing back against Eurocentrism. That tension between self-image and reality is almost written into every corner of the city. Different art museums alone already reveal very different orientations.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/25E94508-0ABE-49E1-890F-E43E02468323_1_105_c.jpeg)

Uruguay, just across the river, feels much more natural. It has no fantasy of being a great power, and it lacks the packaging of a grand narrative. The overall temperament is restrained, inward, and relaxed. It does not seem especially eager to prove anything, nor especially determined to fight its own history.

Chile is noticeably different. It is one of the countries in Latin America that felt most East Asian to me. The state established stable order before society fully did, and long maintained relatively effective governance, which helped make it one of the more developed countries in the region. At the same time, in recent years it has also run into serious public safety problems, to the point that many people seem genuinely anxious. Even on the street, you feel like you can tell who is an undocumented migrant and who looks like a pickpocket.

## Echoes of history

There were also many moments on the trip when I felt a direct resonance with larger history. The strongest one happened in Lima. A civilization as brilliant as the Andes still struggles today to find a way out of the mud of reality, while in the grand churches of the city center, the bones of the Spanish conqueror Pizarro lie quietly on display. Five hundred years ago he led only 168 men and brought down an empire whose army was said to number eighty thousand.

![Pizarro’s remains were apparently not conclusively identified until the mid-20th century, when technology, skeletal injuries, and written records of where he was stabbed were finally put together. Strange and fascinating](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260127000214.png)

Walking along the coast of Lima and watching the sun set over the South Pacific, I suddenly wondered whether Pizarro had once looked at the same view. He came from a humble background, was almost illiterate, had no grand theory, and certainly no noble motivation. And yet after rejection after rejection and failure after failure, he still chose to keep pushing forward, until his own personal fate collided so violently with a crack in history.

![The Costa Verde in Lima, with locals pausing to take in the view](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/80063C56-9E5D-4533-BA4A-8F7E4C6631A6_1_105_c.jpeg)

Seen from today, that destruction of a civilization was of course brutal and bloody. But the very possibility that one individual could shake the course of history still gives me a kind of shock that is hard to put into words. ~~To father a son, let him be like Pizarro~~

Compared with that, such a way of living no longer exists today. Modern society is more complete in its systems, more drawn out in its paths, and individual adventure gets broken down into endless safe steps.

A similar feeling came back to me again and again in Catholic churches across the region. Even though I do not understand theology, and even though these buildings today often stand tangled with colonialism, violence, and oppression, when mass begins and the bells start ringing, I still feel a kind of calm and emotion that is hard to deny. No wonder people a century ago were willing to devote themselves to it so wholeheartedly.

## Connections between people

Meeting and talking with very different kinds of people was just as important a part of the trip.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/8222BA9D-26EC-44B5-AEEF-23C0B23BD1BC_1_105_c.jpeg)

At a hostel in Buenos Aires, I met a 24-year-old Dane who had arrived in Argentina only the day before and planned to live there for a year and start a band. He was the drummer. We talked about things that are funny in our respective cultures, the gap in values between us and our parents’ generation, and our resistance to a conventional life path. It turns out that even in Northern Europe, these quiet expectations from family and society are very much there.

At a Chinese restaurant in Uruguay, I met a Chinese man who had once been posted to South America by a state-owned enterprise, then resigned and stayed to start his own business. He had already spent ten years in the region. Listening to him talk about how Chinese companies actually operate in Latin America filled in a lot of the reality that never appears in books or news.

In Chile, on the way to the airport, the driver and I communicated entirely through a translation app and ended up talking quite a bit about the social differences between China and Chile. Since Santiago does not allow Uber to enter the airport freely, we had to repeatedly rehearse the “background story” and “testimony” in case the police stopped us. The fine, if caught, is apparently 400 U.S. dollars, which is absurd.

![I even took a photo so I wouldn’t forget. Luckily we were not randomly checked in the end](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260127000726.png)

In a hot-air balloon over Teotihuacan, I met a Dutch couple who had just graduated, and only then realized that in Europe, doing a master’s and only then entering the workforce is close to a default path.


![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/D34A4E7A-96B0-4AF6-A01E-AEB2EFB3C41C_1_105_c.jpeg)


And in a hostel in Mexico City, I met two programmers, one from Germany and one from the United States. One had just arrived in Mexico to start working remotely; the other had already been living there remotely for months. Suddenly, a kind of life that had once felt impossible no longer seemed all that distant.

There were also many other stories and encounters that I have not written out here. They really opened up my imagination about what kinds of futures might be possible.


## The “Latin America trap”?

In Chinese-language internet discussions, Latin America is often described as a region that has been “bled dry by the United States” and trapped in long-term suffering. Phrases like the “Latin America trap” come up a lot. Before I left, I carried some version of that assumption as well. Somewhere in the back of my mind, I treated the region as something that needed to be “explained” or even “saved.”

But after traveling through it, I feel more and more that this perspective is simply too careless.

Once basic life is secured, I increasingly doubt whether wealth and achievement really correlate with personal freedom and happiness in the way we so often assume. A lot of suffering may really come from values rather than material conditions.

Maybe there is no single “Latin America trap,” just as there is no single standard by which a society can be judged successful. Treating one country’s or region’s path of development, ordering of values, or way of life as a universal answer may itself be a kind of projection from the outside. **Just as people should not casually judge the kind of life someone else chooses, the same applies between countries and civilizations.**
