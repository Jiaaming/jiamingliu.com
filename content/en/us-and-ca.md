Before coming to Canada, I saw it the way many people do: a gentler version of the United States, quieter, safer, more expensive, and conveniently better for immigration status. After living, working, and traveling here for a while, that impression did seem true on the surface. But the more time I spent here, the more strongly I felt that although the two countries are highly similar in lifestyle, their inner logic is fundamentally different. The difference goes all the way down to how they were built as countries. If you trace them back to the beginning, their underlying logic is almost opposite: the United States was born out of an idealistic impulse, while Canada looks much more like an extension built on practical calculation.

The American story begins with the Mayflower in 1620. A group of Puritans marginalized in English society set out to find a new world where they could worship God freely and escape the pressure of the established church and politics. They were not colonists dispatched by the state. They were believers who chose to leave on their own. Their religious convictions later became part of the core of the American spirit: self-reliance, individual striving, and a faith that hard work can change one’s destiny.

| Puritan belief | Related American spirit | Brief explanation |
|----------------|-------------------------|-------------------|
| Calling | Striving and work ethic | Work is seen as a mission; diligence itself becomes a form of faith. |
| Predestination | Self-proof and worship of success | Achievement becomes proof of being “chosen,” helping form the American Dream. |
| Covenant community | Democracy and social contract | The Mayflower Compact laid the groundwork for self-government and equality. |
| Direct communication with God | Individualism and freedom of thought | Suspicion of authority and respect for personal judgment and broad education. |
| Simplicity and restraint | Pragmatism and worship of efficiency | Emphasis on action, results, and function rather than empty talk. |
| Chosen-people consciousness | American exceptionalism | A belief that America has a unique mission and destiny. |
| Moral community | Public responsibility and rule of law | Social order and civic good are upheld through shared moral expectations. |

To the north, Canada began in a completely different way. Its driving force was not faith but commerce. In 1668, a merchant ship called the *Nonsuch* successfully reached the shores of Hudson Bay and entered the fur trade with the local Cree people. The success of that voyage showed Britain the commercial potential of the north and directly led to the founding of the Hudson’s Bay Company in 1670.

The Hudson’s Bay Company was a business organization. Its employees were mostly merchants, sailors, and hunters, and their goal was to establish trading posts in the wilderness and collect beaver pelts. For them this land was not a promised land. It was an outpost where one could make a living. Because of that, early Canadian development was driven by trade and administrative order rather than faith or revolution.

![A reconstruction of the *Nonsuch* at the Manitoba Museum. It was rebuilt around 1970 and sailed the original route again for two years as a symbol of Anglo-Canadian friendship](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/53D41990-0906-46B9-A2FF-5FE29033C876_1_105_c.jpeg)

That difference in starting point deeply shaped how the two countries developed. American expansion is a history of conquest. From the War of Independence to westward expansion, its territory was won step by step. Canadian expansion, by contrast, feels more like a long sequence of negotiations. From the eastern colonies being combined through Confederation to each westward extension, the process was usually accompanied by agreements with Indigenous peoples:
- The Niagara Treaty in Ontario: in 1764, British representatives and Indigenous peoples around the Great Lakes established a foundation for non-aggression and trade cooperation
- The Red River Agreement in Manitoba: in 1870, it recognized Métis rights and helped bring Manitoba into Confederation
- The numbered treaties across the Prairies: eleven agreements signed between 1871 and 1921 that set out principles for land sharing, compensation, and reserves as the federation expanded westward
  
Even though Indigenous peoples still suffered enormous losses from disease, land seizure, and cultural assimilation, these treaties at least acknowledged their existence and rights at the legal level.

![A reconstruction of the Hudson’s Bay Company office in London at the Manitoba Museum](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/5EB4758B-667E-4E96-81BA-2970ABBC12D9_1_105_c.jpeg)

Canada did not extend to the Pacific through war, but through paperwork and meetings. That habit of expansion by negotiation rather than conquest helped shape its moderate political character.

That same character also appears in its bilingualism and multiculturalism. After Britain defeated France, it did not completely erase French culture by force, and that meant the country had to learn compromise from the beginning. English and French coexisted, Indigenous land rights were acknowledged, and multiculturalism was eventually written into law. These things are less a matter of modern “political correctness” than the practical inheritance of how Canada was formed.

A political culture built on compromise allowed Canada to absorb elements from both the Anglo and French traditions. It carries the Anglo-American respect for individual freedom and markets, while also incorporating the French emphasis on the public good and the role of the state.

| Tradition | Core value | Role of the state | Social idea |
|-----------|------------|-------------------|-------------|
| **Anglo system** | Individual freedom, contract, and self-government | Limited intervention, emphasis on markets and rule of law | Stresses personal responsibility and competition |
| **French system** | Public interest, equality, and order | The state leads social development and resource distribution | Stresses collective welfare and social solidarity |
| **Canadian compromise** | Consensus and pluralism | The state protects markets while also maintaining the public good | Produces a moderate, inclusive middle path |

So when Americans talk about the American Dream and celebrate individual struggle, Canadians are more likely to talk about how to improve public welfare and secure a decent floor for everyone. The United States is a melting pot, hoping different cultures will fuse into a single American spirit. Canada is more like a mosaic, allowing each culture to keep its own distinct shape.

In Canada, diversity itself is a national value. That is its biggest difference from the United States, and in some ways from all developed countries: the United States creates power through unity, while Canada maintains balance through diversity. In a world that is moving rightward, that kind of balance feels especially valuable.

Of course, the cost of that balance is a possible lack of momentum for deep change. Because the country was not built through revolution, Canadian society also lacks some of that impulse to smash the old order. In the technological age, American individualism and appetite for risk are more likely to generate disruptive innovation, and many of Canada’s top people eventually head south in search of bigger opportunities.

In a sense, the two countries complement one another. The United States stands for speed, competition, and idealism. Canada stands for stability, negotiation, and institutionalization.
