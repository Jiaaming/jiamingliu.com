As I write this, I’m sitting in the common room of a hostel in Montevideo. Outside, the rain and wind have not stopped. At last I have the time to sit down and properly sort through these dreamlike three weeks.

I actually hesitated for a long time over whether I should write down my feelings right now. Every single day of the trip was so good that it made me almost cautious, as if failing to capture it in the most natural and sincere language would itself be a kind of betrayal, even a kind of desecration.  

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260110192949.png)

Antarctica is a beautiful place. I’ve never been especially good at describing scenery, so I won’t pretend otherwise. The moment that stays with me most is the night of camping: standing on a hilltop at sunset, the whole world was so quiet that I could hear the sound of glaciers far away cracking and falling into the sea. The way the sunset, icebergs, and ocean reflected one another was a hundred times more overwhelming than any Antarctica photo I managed to take. Unless one day I get to watch a sunset on Mars, which I think should be possible, I probably won’t forget that scene for the rest of my life.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260110183452.png)

Talking with the people I met on the ship was the biggest gain of the trip. As one of the youngest people on the expedition ship, I often sat in a corner of the dining room or on the deck, listening to passengers ten or several decades older than me talk about their lives.

One person described Jerusalem at dawn, and said that when he saw the smoke rising from the Mount of Olives, he understood for the first time the force of religion. Another mentioned the Taj Mahal at four in the morning, and said the marble looked unreal that day...

Those stories were not always dramatic, and maybe that is why they felt so real. There were also the stories of their own lives, the choices they made, the compromises they accepted, the things they eventually came to terms with. It didn’t feel like I was merely hearing stories. It felt more like I was briefly stepping into their lives, feeling the time they had lived through, standing on the lands they had once stood on.

![On the ship we would talk almost every night until very late.](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260110183815.png)

In many uninterrupted moments, I had a very rare feeling: it was as if I possessed both youth and the feeling of youth at the same time. No amount of literature, film, or art on this planet could have given me that exact experience. No worldly money, power, or women could have produced that kind of shock either, though to be fair I have never had any of the three, haha.

In my [2023 year-end review](http://localhost:5173/#/posts/2023-summary), I wrote that I already felt like I had somehow lived into the state of being twenty-six or twenty-seven. But at that moment, I could also clearly feel myself returning to sixteen or seventeen.
 
For a long time, I had been unable to let go of the years from 2022 to 2024. Even though exchange programs, research, and work had taken me across China, the United States, and Canada, and through different companies and cities, which was already a lot for an undergraduate, I still carried an almost existential kind of fear inside me.

In extreme solitude, day after day, I often felt like a small boat drifting on a vast sea. No one could point out a direction for me anymore, and no one was really moving alongside me. It seemed that I could go anywhere, and yet also that I could arrive nowhere. The waves of uncertainty always felt ready to swallow me.

Before 2022, I had always thought of myself as someone who loved life, and who was willing to actively catch hold of it. Whenever the sun was setting and the evening light came through the dorm window, I would always go out to the balcony, look at the changing sky, and take a photo. I still have hundreds of those pictures on my phone.    

![This was basically the angle every time](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/EA9AB746-3283-4C16-A3EC-201195DDE268_1_201_a.jpeg)

But after that, and all the way until I graduated from undergrad, I rarely felt the urge to photograph things just to remember them. It was not that life had become boring. It was more that I seemed to have lost some instinctive connection with the world. Things still happened, but I mostly just watched them happen, and rarely left anything behind, even though I went to many distant countries and faraway cities. None of those places made me feel relaxed. I felt myself slowly becoming a rather dull person.

Even back then, I knew very clearly that this closed-off state was not reasonable, and yet I was never able to change it. Those same three years also overlapped with the bursting of the global tech bubble, the harshest wave of layoffs, and an overseas job market that had entered what people could honestly call hell mode. I had no choice but to seal myself inside an airtight suit of armor, block out external feeling, and keep running as hard as I could.

I remember that during my internship, on evenings when I came back to the dorm around seven or eight, I would buy two jidan guanbing near the back gate of the school, one with spicy strips and one with shredded potato, and eat them while walking back in the dark, all the while asking myself whether I could really keep walking down this road.

That state did not really loosen until the early morning of March 17, 2024, the day I finished my research internship in Canada and left Edmonton. Around that point, I had confirmed where I was going for my master’s and had also secured my Microsoft internship offer. The night before, I made dumplings and had dinner with my host grandmother and a newly arrived Chinese immigrant sister in Canada. It was one of the few experiences like that I had had in years.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/AA4A458E-1BFF-408B-B0C6-064DC7F5EF15_1_105_c.jpeg)


At four in the morning, I put my suitcase in the living room, called the Uber, and sat alone on the sofa. The yellow light lit up the empty room, and outside it was still snowing. I just looked at everything in silence, and suddenly felt a long-forgotten sadness of departure, as if the gate inside me that had been held tight for so long to block off emotion had finally begun to loosen.


In Antarctica, on the sea, in South America, among strange cities and strangers, I felt that long-lost state again: I was in the present. I walked, looked, listened, and recorded things once in a while. Gradually, all my old regret about time, all my fear of the unknown, all my anger at losing control, and all my attachment to unfinished things seemed to dissolve like fireworks in the air, without leaving any clear trace.

![On New Year’s Eve we counted down on the deck with fireworks, and it even started snowing. The first snow of 2026](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20260110183344.png)


I’m not sure what these experiences will leave behind in the future. Maybe they will only slowly reveal themselves over the next several years. Maybe they won’t change my direction. Maybe they won’t immediately bring any concrete result. But that no longer feels important.

The wind and rain are still going on outside, while the common room is gradually becoming quiet. I don’t know what will happen ahead, and I’m not in a hurry to know. Once the storm stops, I’ll just keep moving.
