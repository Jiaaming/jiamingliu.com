Last month in Guadalajara, I wandered through a few produce markets and department centers that mostly locals visit. The atmosphere reminded me strongly of the old shopping centers I used to go to in Beijing as a kid: dim yellow lighting, narrow aisles where you had to turn sideways to pass, and air filled with the smell of raw meat, spices, and cheap textiles.

But one detail caught me off guard: nearly every shop displayed its Instagram account. Walking on the street, the same thing felt even more obvious. Whether it was young people waiting for a bus or a taco vendor frying food, most people were staring down at their phone screens.

![You often see anti-American graffiti in Mexico](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/B30A9870-386A-4B61-9004-6BBCADF32F22_1_105_c.jpeg)

That kind of “digital intrusion” made me feel that the influence of American companies on Mexico, or more broadly the influence of developed countries on developing ones, goes far beyond industrial transfer or trade surplus. The deeper layer of extraction is happening at the level of attention. Billions of users hand over several hours of focus every day to apps like Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube.

Of course, technology itself is neutral. But this one-way flow of attention is very real.

## The expansion logic of software companies

Why are these companies able to penetrate such small corners of daily life across the world? Behind it is a mechanism of expansion that is completely different from traditional industry.

Traditional manufacturing is constrained by the physical world. Whether you want to build a car factory or a clothing production line, you need huge fixed-asset investment, factories, distribution, and logistics. The speed is limited by nature.

But tech companies are different. They often burn money in the early stage, but once the business model works, they can sweep through a country’s market very quickly. Uber is a textbook example. Founded in 2009, it was already operating in 58 countries by 2015. The strategy is simple and brutal: occupy the market before regulation catches up. By the time taxi drivers are protesting in the streets and governments start writing new rules, Uber already has tens of millions of users and becomes almost impossible to remove.

These tech giants have built a flywheel that more or less runs on itself: gain monopoly in one area, like Google in search, AWS in cloud, or Meta in social networks -> make astronomical profits -> use those profits to pour more money into R&D and talent -> pull further ahead technically -> strengthen the monopoly even more. The more monopolistic they become, the more money they make; the more money they make, the wider the gap becomes. Because software is not constrained by tariffs or physical space, these companies are not limited by raw materials, shipping, or production lines in the way traditional businesses are. Once that monopolistic structure forms, it becomes almost irreversible.

## Power shifting from land to data

To me, these tech giants feel a lot like the [East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company) four hundred years ago. Back then, those companies had private armies, could wage war, sign treaties with sovereign states, and operate across much of the planet. In India alone it commanded more than 200,000 private soldiers, stronger than many European countries at the time. Local officials and tax systems fell under its control. It was arguably stronger than today’s biggest listed tech companies combined, and probably the most powerful corporation in human history.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251209121202.png)

Today’s tech giants have no physical armies, but they are carrying out another kind of colonization in virtual space and capital markets. They hold more detailed user data than any state, control channels through which information flows, and possess wealth on the scale of nations. Even antitrust fines worth billions of euros from the EU feel more like routine operating costs to them. They maintain huge legal teams whose job is to study and exploit the lag in national regulation, and even force sovereign states to rewrite the rules in response.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251209112707.png)

These companies are also becoming increasingly international. More than half of Google’s employees are non-American, and 53% of its revenue comes from outside the United States. Meta and Microsoft are similar. ByteDance wants something similar too, but TikTok’s political impact is too large for it to remain untouched. In other words, no single government can easily control these firms anymore.

Governments are aware of this too, which is why we now see China’s antitrust regulation of Alibaba, the U.S. breakup case against Google, and the EU’s Digital Markets Act.

That does not mean governments naturally stand on the side of “the people.” Regulation is mainly about taking power back, especially new forms of power like data, narrative control, and taxation. The boundary between governments and large corporations is not always clear.

## Is this really a bad thing?

Today the efficiency and execution capacity of capital machines have already far surpassed those of states. It sounds cyberpunk and dystopian, but is it necessarily bad?

Not long ago I listened to [Lex Fridman’s podcast](https://lexfridman.com/jeff-bezos/) with Jeff Bezos about Blue Origin. Bezos said, “Humanity will eventually move into space. It is not a question of whether, but when.” Last year I read [Musk’s biography](https://book.douban.com/subject/36518892/), where he said something similar: if humanity cannot become a multi-planet species, we may eventually destroy ourselves through resource exhaustion, war, or some other great filter. That idea is one of the forces driving SpaceX.

My own impression is that many Silicon Valley founders carry a strong flavor of [accelerationism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_accelerationism): the purpose of civilization is not to make every individual happier, but to push technology forward as hard as possible under the pressure of competition and capital. There will inevitably be failure in that process, and there will inevitably be sacrifice. Some industries disappear. Some groups, maybe even most people, lose out. But eventually quantitative change becomes qualitative change, and civilization moves to a higher level.

In a certain sense, history does seem to validate that logic again and again. The Industrial Revolution threw countless craftspeople out of work and forced workers into horrible factory conditions, but two hundred years later average life expectancy has doubled and extreme poverty has fallen dramatically.

The internet revolution is similar. Physical bookstores, newspapers, and record shops disappeared in large numbers, but now anyone can access enormous amounts of knowledge for free, and creators can reach audiences across the world directly. Technological progress always comes with pain, but in the long run it does seem to improve overall human welfare.

## The narrative trap of capital

But just like the grand narrative traps created by states, technology improves civilization as a whole, not every individual.

Did the workers in Manchester textile mills, who started working at twelve and died of lung disease at thirty, live long enough to see that “civilizational leap”? What exactly did the street vendor in a Mexican market gain after his attention was harvested by Instagram for hours a day? What happens to ordinary drivers, translators, customer support workers, or programmers once AI starts replacing them?

The monopoly of tech giants will continue to deepen. AI will continue to develop. Some people will be sacrificed, and some will benefit. We cannot stop that.

But as individuals, we can still do a few things to protect ourselves:

- Move back and forth across different companies and systems to maximize your own gains. Go wherever the treatment is better, the opportunities are greater, and life feels more comfortable.
- Maintain sovereignty over your own attention. Reduce addictive content consumption and become more of a value creator.
- If you believe in these companies, invest in them. If big tech is going to keep monopolizing and keep making money, then buy the stocks. At the very least, make yourself one of the beneficiaries of the system as well.
