In early November, I embarked on a journey to Mexico, visiting **Guadalajara** and **Mexico City (CDMX)** - the country's second and largest cities respectively.

---

Under the rendering of modern popular culture, Mexico is often stereotyped as a country where drug dealers are rampant. My initial impression of Mexico was also based on the lawless land where gangs are rampant in Villeneuve's ["Sicario"](https://movie.douban.com/subject/25881247/).

In fact, violent crimes against tourists have actually subsided in recent years. But this is not because the drug dealers disappeared, but because of a tacit understanding based on interests:

1. **Economic Accounts**: Tourism contributes nearly 10% of Mexico’s GDP. For drug gangs, they not only profit from drugs, but also launder money by investing in resorts, bars and hotels. The more tourists there are, the greater the legal cash flow and the easier it is to launder money.
2. **Lightning Rod Effect**: Once a foreign tourist (especially an American) is killed, it will inevitably attract strong pressure from the National Guard of the federal government and even the United States. This is the "business interference" that drug lords least want to see.

Therefore, Mexico has formed an extremely absurd security paradox: as a tourist, the area you are in is often "protected" by the government and gangs. Data shows that the probability of tourists being murdered in Mexico is only one percent of that of local residents. Even in some tourist areas, the safety level of night travel is almost the same as that of large cities in the United States.

These heavy topics aside, Mexico is a stunning kaleidoscope of culture. This land is not just one civilization, different regions have different styles, and even before the arrival of the Spanish, completely different worlds coexisted here.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251119230928.png)


Mexico has three main ancient civilization systems:

| Civilization system | Representative regions | Characteristics |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Central Plateau Civilization (Aztec)** | Mexico City, Teotihuacan | The city is large in scale, advocates force and conquest, has strong centralization, and is famous for human sacrifice. |
| **Mayan Civilization** | Yucatan Peninsula, Guatemala | A city-state political system similar to ancient Greece, with extremely complex hieroglyphics, and peak attainments in astronomy and calendars. |
| **Western Mexico Civilization (Occidente)** | Guadalajara, Colima, Michoacán | Unique tomb-style shaft tombs, without giant pyramids, but the pottery civilization is extremely developed and pays more attention to life and the afterlife. |

Most people in the world are familiar with the first two: although the Aztecs were glorious, they were completely destroyed by the Spanish (today's Mexico City is built on its ruins); while the Mayan civilization, although the city-state has declined, its language and blood are still passed down in Yucatan and Guatemala.

The first stop of my trip, **Guadalajara**, is located on the soil of **Western Mexico**; the subsequent **Mexico City** is the heart of the **Aztec**. There is a huge difference between the two cities alone.


# AIRPORT ADVENTURES

A few months ago, while browsing the official website of Flair, a well-known Canadian low-cost airline, I accidentally discovered a direct route from Vancouver to Guadalajara. The original plan was just to travel to and from this place for a short stay, but later I learned that there are nearly one hundred US dollars in entry and departure related taxes and fees in Mexico (including tourist tax and airport usage fee TUA, etc.). So I also added Mexico City to this itinerary, so that the "ticket money" would not be wasted.

![Arrived at Guadalajara Customs in the evening](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/7DCA9B67-8D8C-447A-A881-916D6FEE9924_1_105_c.jpeg)

When I did my homework before, I thought Uber was only not allowed in typical rip-off areas like Cancun, but I didn’t expect that the same was true in Guatemala.

I originally planned to call a taxi directly at the airport, but I changed drivers several times. After taking the order, everyone sent mysterious messages asking all kinds of strange questions (such as "How many of them?"). When I didn't answer, the other party canceled the order directly.

![After wasting almost ten minutes, the driver in the picture above came to take the order and told me to sit in the front passenger seat to avoid the "National Guard"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/C9BA3DFE-9126-4DFF-87B4-79C2814B4716_1_105_c.jpeg)

After I got in the car, I habitually tried to pull the seat belt, but I couldn't pull it out after pulling hard several times. I asked the driver a few words in English and found that he didn't understand what I was saying at all. Only then did I notice that the driver himself was not wearing a seat belt at all. I had no choice but to give up and thought to myself, "No, can Mexico not wear seat belts..."

Then the driver gestured and told me: If anyone asks, just say I am his friend. I was full of suspicion and wanted to ask him what happened, but seeing his poor English and nervous expression, I had no choice but to give up.

About five or six minutes after the car drove out of the airport, a rapid siren suddenly came from behind. A National Guard vehicle actually rushed up and forced us to park on the side of the road. Four or five police officers gathered around and interrogated the driver for almost ten minutes... In the middle of it, the driver even had to put out the engine.

During this period, I was so confused that I didn’t dare to take any photos. Later, a policeman came over, smiled and shook my hand (the policemen I met in Mexico are quite polite), and then asked me straight to the point if I was an Uber and where I was going.

My catastrophic thinking had already reached the point where I imagined being caught lying and being caught in the police station, so I decisively chose to confess and get leniency. After hearing this, the police did not give me any trouble. They just asked me to cancel the trip and told me that this was a federal jurisdiction and that online taxi hailing was not allowed. They asked me to go outside the airport and hail a taxi.

![Later, I followed the sporadic flow of people to the location indicated by the police and took a taxi again. I found that there were many people waiting for the bus here. ](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/05E34989-D3D0-4A6C-A984-DAA1CC1DF840_1_105_c.jpeg)

After checking the information, I realized the reason: It turns out that there is a powerful taxi union in Mexico. They have divided so-called "franchise areas" (mainly airports and bus stations) and strictly prohibit online taxis from entering to grab business. After all, the price of online taxis is often only half of that of taxis. Guadalajara is a relatively strict city, while Mexico City is relatively loose.

As for the **National Guard** that forced us to stop, this is a paramilitary police force established by the current Mexican President Lopez in 2019. It is designed to replace the highly corrupt federal police. Its main task is to combat drug trafficking groups and maintain public security. But in places like airports, they seem to be more often engaged in "formalistic" enforcement, mainly to cope with pressure from labor unions.

It is said that the police generally just pretend that if the passengers insist that they are friends, they will let them go. And even if the result is a lie, there is actually no legal liability (there is no law for passengers), but the driver will probably get a ticket - but recalling his behavior of not even wearing a seat belt, this brother's risk tolerance must be extremely high.

According to other people’s comments on the Internet later, I learned that this is also a small probability event. Many people take taxis at the airport without anyone checking. I didn’t expect that I would win the jackpot on my first visit, which was interesting.

![The delicious tacos we ate at the hotel near the hostel at night cost about US$13 (including tip)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/FC0315E0-F2B1-41AD-B3A6-2E6CD9A55A03_1_105_c.jpeg)

The cleanliness of tap water throughout Mexico is very limited. It is absolutely not allowed to drink directly, and even mouthwash is not recommended. Guadalajara is located on the Mexican Plateau, with an altitude of about 2,200 meters (similar to Kunming), and water sources are scarce. Here, you basically need to buy water. There are few places to collect water in airports and museums, and restaurants do not provide free drinking water.

# The City of the Missing

Early in the morning on the first day, I started walking from the youth hostel to the city.

The first thing that shocked me along the way was not the exotic buildings, but the countless missing persons notices. And the closer you go to the city center, the denser the posts are, but you can see them less in the residential areas you pass by.

Jalisco state, where Guadalajara is located, is one of the areas with the most missing persons in Mexico. According to statistics, more than 110,000 people are listed as missing in Mexico, and Jalisco state accounts for a very high proportion. This is often related to active local drug cartels - especially the notorious "Jalisco New Generation Cartel" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jalisco_New_Generation_Cartel).

Civil rights groups intentionally placed these signs in the most visible places downtown as a silent protest against government inaction. What is worth pondering is that since the Mexican democratic system gradually took shape in 2000, officials have maintained a high level of tolerance for such expressions-here, the risk to freedom of speech does not come from the government, but from the drug cartels and regional gangs hiding in the shadows.

Went all the way to the city center. As one of Spain's most influential overseas colonies, the urban planning here strictly follows the Spanish style: with a central square (Plaza) as the core, surrounded by cathedrals representing religious power and government palaces representing secular power.


![Downtown Square](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/13E63416-7B52-4AAC-8704-A04EF299EC0E_1_105_c.jpeg)

![Must go inside the church every time](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/A78E8FA1-A32D-44C2-9394-52872347F9B3_1_105_c.jpeg)

This is completely different from the planning logic of North American cities. In the United States or Canada, downtown is usually a modern high-rise building and business district (CBD), which turns into a ghost town at night; while wealthy areas and living areas are often located in suburbs.

But in Mexico, the old city is still the beating heart of culture. Although the rich may have moved to the suburbs, the square is still the center for socialization and leisure for all citizens. This was especially evident during my subsequent experience in Mexico City.

![State government, usually free to enter and visit, and you can also use the restroom](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/E496D6CF-EEBA-4421-8796-D87A274F5E9D_1_105_c.jpeg)

# Tlaquepaque
After a quick lunch, I took the subway to **Tlaquepaque**.

![Waiting for the bus](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/C63B7B60-6D66-4CC9-BC60-293D75466748_1_105_c.jpeg)


There are three rail transit lines in Guadalajara. I took the latest **Line 3**, which will only be opened in 2020. A ride only costs 10 pesos (about 0.5 US dollars), with no limit on distance. After purchasing the ticket, you will receive a receipt with a QR code, scan the code and enter the station. Compared with many old subways in Europe and the United States, the subways here are very modern and clean, operate with minimal noise and are very fast, not inferior to China's subways.

![Inside the carriage](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/66F66867-11EE-4844-B3FD-D64A5FDA0981_1_105_c.jpeg)

Guadalajara is actually a sprawling metropolitan area made up of several municipalities.
Tlaquepaque was formerly an independent pottery town, famous for its traditional crafts, pottery and mariachi bands, and its streets are lined with colorful colonial buildings.

![The buildings on the streets of Tlaquepaque are mostly colorful](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251118234734.png)

A simple rule of thumb to recognize when you've crossed a borough boundary is to look at the layout: if you see a new central square, a cathedral, and a town hall, you've probably arrived in another "city."

![The square and church in downtown Tlaquepaque](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/F1D9F316-7103-47ED-95D7-321D9628D03B_1_105_c.jpeg)

# Zapopan
After that, I took the metro to **Zapopan** and wandered around.


![Viewing the city skyline from the subway platform](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/95CE6D68-2D3B-43D2-A2BE-1B47F8AA01EC_1_105_c.jpeg)

Zapopan is a combination of wealth and faith. It is one of the wealthiest municipalities in the Guadalajara metropolitan area, with a large number of modern shopping malls and high-end residences.

![Even here, the ZPN (abbreviation for Zapopan) sign is covered with missing person notices, a constant reminder of the shadow behind this land](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/8EBF1051-7911-4964-8C23-D1B062C0D992_1_105_c.jpeg)



![Basilica of Our Lady of Zapopan](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/9012485C-0E47-4CA1-A1BF-35AEEA281EF0_1_105_c.jpeg)

The landmark here is the Basilica of Our Lady of Zapopan. This church is dedicated to the famous "Our Lady of Zapopan". Every year on October 12, more than two million believers will participate in a grand procession to welcome the statue of Our Lady back here from the Guadalajara Cathedral. It is one of the largest religious events in Mexico.

![The interior of Zapopan City Hall basically retains the colonial architectural style](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/2CC5A501-7EDD-44AD-B115-3ADDB0263FC5_1_105_c.jpeg)

In this area, I unexpectedly found a film developing shop. The price for flushing a roll is 150 pesos (approximately RMB 58). Although it is three times more expensive than in China (about 18 yuan), considering the sky-high price of 200 yuan (40 Canadian dollars) for a roll in Canada, this price seems extremely affordable and cost-effective.

Even more interesting was the chat with the shop owner’s grandma. She told me that her lover’s ancestors were Chinese and their surname was **Woo**. Because there is no "W" sound in Spanish pronunciation, it is pronounced like **Bo**.


# Museums and Colonial Architecture

The next day, my trip revolved around the museum. Mexico’s museums have two very conscientious features: first, most of them are free of charge (or extremely cheap); second, most of them are converted from well-preserved large-scale colonial buildings. Even if you can’t understand the exhibits, just admiring the sense of space and light and shadow of the building itself is worth the price of admission.

It is a pity that the curatorial ideas here are very "localized", and there are very few exhibition halls with English explanations. They all rely on guesswork and translation software.

![Museo Regional de Guadalajara](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/EC6B9B9F-2503-4109-888C-3C8D9C967A72_1_105_c.jpeg)

The first stop is the **Guadalajara Regional Museum**. Built in the 18th century, it was originally a seminary and later served as a prison during the Revolutionary War.

Guadalajara has been an important center of the Viceroyalty of New Spain since its founding in 1542. Compared to Mexico City, which was built on Aztec ruins, it retains a purer Spanish colonial color while also integrating the unique cowboy culture of Jalisco.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/C98C0DED-E8C2-43BC-8F40-B61918F09B93_1_105_c.jpeg)

While walking in the city center, I bumped into a 22-year-old Mexican guy. Seeing that I looked Chinese, he took the initiative to talk to me. He can speak a few words of Chinese and is quite fluent in English.

During the chat, we learned that he plans to study abroad in Tianjin or Shanghai next year and plans to major in Asian studies. I added him on WeChat and saw that his WeChat avatar was an illustration with the theme of China-Mexico trade, so I guessed that he wanted to engage in foreign trade-related industries. This is very reasonable - China is currently Mexico's second largest trading partner, and Mexico is also China's second largest trading partner in Latin America. In the context of Sino-US decoupling, there are indeed many opportunities.

He reminded me that Mexico is still somewhat dangerous, and I should be more vigilant when traveling and not go to remote places.


The highlight of the day is the **Cabañas Workhouse**.

![Hospicio Cabañas](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/3B5A4007-D084-4D39-AD9F-2A331C765F72_1_105_c.jpeg)

Listed as a World Cultural Heritage by the United Nations, this building was built in the early 19th century and was originally a "house of mercy" for orphans, the elderly and the disabled. The architectural style is typical neoclassical, with 23 courtyards, extremely grand and symmetrical.

But what makes it world-famous is the 57 giant murals left here by José Clemente Orozco, one of the three masters of Mexican murals.

![Hospicio Cabañas](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/6CD8BEEC-66A2-4B65-9CAE-43788E588079_1_105_c.jpeg)

According to the commentator, Orozco used a lot of symmetrical structures and metaphors in his design, but the content he expressed was terrifying. Especially the masterpiece on the dome, the Burning Man, and the surrounding depictions of Spanish conquistadors (Cortes was depicted as a half-man, half-machine monster) and oppressed natives.

The concept conveyed by the work is extremely close to "nihilism": history seems to be just a cycle of endless suffering, and neither Spanish colonists nor modern civilization can solve mankind's existential crisis. This sense of despair is tragically consistent with the unfortunate fate of Latin America.

The most ironic and fascinating thing is that these very critical, even anarchistic works of art are funded by the Mexican government. When the Mexican government, especially the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI), was in power, it censored literary and artistic expressions extremely loosely, and even used this to flaunt its revolutionary orthodoxy. This magical operation of "official funding and scolding the official" is probably one of the reasons why Mexican culture is so charming.

![Museo del Ejército y Fuerza Aérea](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/90FB83ED-AE23-4561-94EE-93FB137D8A5B_1_105_c.jpeg)

Then I visited the **Mexico Army and Air Force Museum**. It displays many weapons and armaments from different periods after the beginning of colonization, as well as a lot of military education content and praises of military glory.

This place is under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of National Defense, the staff are all active military personnel, and the atmosphere is obviously much more serious.

Here is a brief introduction to Mexico’s unique **“military-police binary opposition”**:
In Mexico, the police force is usually under the control of local governments, has low wages, poor equipment, and is easily infiltrated and bribed by local gangs, so it is discredited in the hearts of the people;

The **military** takes orders directly from the federation and has heavy equipment and relatively good discipline. Therefore, when fighting drug lords, the Mexican government often distrusts the police and directly dispatches the military. This is why the heavily armed patrols you see on the streets are often the National Guard or regular troops, rather than ordinary police.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/FECA0353-2CC8-4B0C-B255-8A1CD2D9FA9B_1_105_c.jpeg)

I've always been curious about military/police museums in different countries. Because such museums often display the country's purest and most unabashed official ideology. Through the setting of the exhibition hall content - which glory is emphasized and which failure is ignored - we can often get a glimpse of the national narrative that the rulers want to shape.


Out of the museum and back onto the streets. Along the way I saw many shops selling snacks or small commodities.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/678D1B4E-0256-405C-AB9C-E85F69A243D8_1_105_c.jpeg)

The business ecology here confuses me: the crowds passing by are bustling, and most of them seem to be local residents rather than tourists, but the items sold in the shops are surprisingly homogeneous—all souvenirs, hats, boots, and plastic toys. Not only are the positioning similar, but the target customer groups are also confusing, as if the entire market is in a self-sufficient internal cycle.

![The internal department store feels very much like the Beijing wholesale market in the early 21st century, very dreamlike](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/78B5F9DF-E241-4CE2-B3DA-9787FC5ECCCA_1_105_c.jpeg)
