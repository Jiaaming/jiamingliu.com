## Singapore

The direct flight to Singapore takes a total of 16 hours from Vancouver, which is second only to the 18-hour round-trip flight from New York to Singapore among global commercial routes. I thought it would be difficult, but I took two melatonin tablets on the plane and slept for about 12 hours.

It was 7 a.m. when we landed, and we took the subway from the airport to the youth hostel in the city. At about eight o'clock, there were already many foreign workers on the subway who had come to work in the city. Most of them were South Asians who were simply dressed and tired. They were in sharp contrast to the local white-collar workers. Of course, Singapore’s institutional inequality in its treatment of foreign workers is nothing new.   

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/4287D06F-2B53-4BE2-95CE-150D2A8A57C5_1_105_c.jpeg)

The original plan was to stay in Singapore for three days and two nights - mainly to allow enough time to catch up on the jet lag, but I didn't expect that the jet lag was adjusted on the first day, and I even walked more than 20,000 steps in one go that day, which really surprised me.        

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/DA6F2D28-F553-4363-84AA-7CD5ADFE2019_1_105_c.jpeg)

The museum in Singapore is very interactive and educational, and the layout is very fascinating. It is especially detailed about the challenges and struggles faced during the British and Japanese rule and later independence. In addition, I also learned a lot about the overall history of Southeast Asia.    


![National Museum of Singapore](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/6C927E67-8EBA-4D00-BC56-6C1A1455A7CF_1_105_c.jpeg)


In addition to the National Museum of Singapore, I also went to the [Old Ford Factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Former_Ford_Factory), a relatively unpopular place in the west. 

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/2592E051-1FDB-43AC-B66D-BEE0C1AA2052_1_105_c.jpeg)

This is the place where the documents were signed when the British surrendered to the Japanese in February 1941. It has now been transformed into an exhibition hall. It introduces the modern history of Singapore in great detail, telling the development under the British colonial rule, the situation before and after the Japanese occupation, and the story of the return and independence.     

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/C7D539AA-858B-4337-AF35-FF716044F61F_1_105_c.jpeg)


The next day I happened to catch up with the opening day of The Istina, the presidential palace - it is usually only open two or three times a year. I was lucky enough to catch it. Met the President of Singapore [Tharman Shanmugaratnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tharman_Shanmugaratnam) and the president’s wife; we stayed for about half an hour for various interactions and visits.   

![The Istana and Sri Temasek](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/EE09D16E-BAD2-4487-814D-D3A8873312D6_1_105_c.jpeg)

Tharman Shanmugaratnam is the president with the highest popular vote rate (70.4%) in Singapore's history and has high support among all ethnic groups. [The president’s wife](https://wiki.sg/p/Jane_Yumiko_Ittogi) is also of mixed Chinese and Japanese descent, and can indeed be regarded as a representative of “cross-ethnic identity”. 

![Marina Bay Sands](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/2F89B7C7-2180-4B72-A268-98285420E976_1_105_c.jpeg)

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/9774507E-6098-4284-9673-3D449F0FCD47_1_105_c.jpeg)

The overall cost-effectiveness of eating in Singapore feels very average (the few Hainanese chicken rice meals were not as delicious as the ones I had at [Singapore Restaurant](https://singaporehawker.ca/) in Vancouver...). The overall price of non-Singaporean dishes is on the expensive side and the taste is average.   

## Kuala Lumpur

![Singapore->Kuala Lumpur](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/C68DFC9E-61EB-49AF-BD2A-61DB5E5EECE0_1_105_c.jpeg)

Considering the short distance between Singapore and Kuala Lumpur, I chose to take a bus to enter the country. Except when I was a kid, I took a bus with my parents for a tour group. I had never taken one myself, which was quite a novel experience.

In the car, I helped an old Malaysian man open his lunch box. On the way, he gave me a kind of magical beans, but they tasted very bitter and astringent, but he ate them with gusto. Finally returned it.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20250809142906.png)

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20250417132312.png)

The security check was smooth and casual. When I asked if the film could be hand-checked, the customs just asked me to take it over. Maybe there are only tourists arriving from Singapore here.    

Kuala Lumpur's public transportation system is very complicated. The transportation payment method is still stuck in the traditional card swiping method (and buses do not yet support cash and coins), and different lines may require different bus cards. Not only is it unfriendly to tourists, but many times locals are also confused.    

Taking a taxi is really cheap. Although Kuala Lumpur has a car-oriented urban design, it is always very congested. Several times it took 10 minutes to take the bus and half an hour to wait for the bus. I asked the local driver and he said it had always been like this, nothing more.   

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/D9CD0675-AF22-48CB-8D8B-CCAD04AD7EB1_4_5005_c.jpeg)

The comparison between Kuala Lumpur and Singapore is also interesting. Both cities are full of high-rise buildings. If Singapore is clean, tidy but orderly and lifeless, then Kuala Lumpur is lively, pyrotechnic, but a little chaotic and wild.   

I went to a relatively niche [Royal Malaysian Police Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Malaysian_Police_Museum). Exhibiting many weapons and equipment related to the opposition/terrorists (CPM) at that time, the museum is worthy of being a country's ideological echo chamber.       

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/46853579-650F-4D46-A918-9EB392258B46_4_5005_c.jpeg)


What was more interesting was that at the museum I met a middle-aged man who was working in Saudi Arabia and a top backpacker who had just graduated and was going to work in Japan, so we formed a group to visit for a day. The latter’s interest and love for history was truly jaw-dropping to me. One of his travel goals was to visit all the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Halls in the world (I found that Sun Yat-sen's influence seemed to be much greater than what was stated in high school history books), and he got a lot of information about Taiwan from him.


Maybe it’s because I haven’t exercised for too long. My left leg got a little strained after walking around in Singapore for two days. I also walked a lot in the first two days in Kuala Lumpur and it didn’t get better. So I stayed around my accommodation (a place quite close to the city center) to do some activities. I focused on visiting nearby bookstores and browsing some books and reviews related to national politics and history.   

During this period, I learned about an unavoidable event in Malaysian history—the “May 13 Incident” ethnic conflict in 1969. Before that, Malaysia was already controlled by the Malays, and the constitution (Article 153) had stipulated that Malays should be given priority in exchange for the citizenship rights of the Chinese and Indians at that time.

![Muzium Negara](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/F4CE7562-21AD-46FE-B501-5AB9B39F9B6B_1_105_c.jpeg)

The reasons for the conflict are widely debated. Government officials tend to describe it as a conflict with the Communist Party of Malaysia, in order to maintain national stability; especially in the Royal Police Museum, it focuses on the items seized from the Communist Party of Malaysia during the emergency period. The main concern among the people is still the long-term economic inequality between ethnic groups, and the long-simmering conflicts finally broke out. After that, the Malaysian government launched the "New Economic Policy" to further support Malays and other disadvantaged groups.     

It is said that this matter is a red line for the Malaysian authorities. Because the various national narratives and historical changes involved are too complicated, the general approach is to suppress relevant discussions. They are only used during political campaigns to attract attention and to warn and intimidate. Kuala Lumpur also has few related attractions or monuments.    

But when I was in the bookstore, I also read many Chinese books about the May 13 incident. For example, there are memoirs of May 13 based on interviews with people who experienced it, and there are books analyzing policy issues in Malaysia. Personally, I feel that there is no extremely serious censorship. Of course, this may also be related to the fact that Malaysia's current political situation is still unstable and political parties change frequently.     

This made me think about the fact that racial issues left over from history are often structural contradictions that are difficult to resolve. The "Malay first" clause in the constitution will inevitably affect the sense of belonging of other ethnic groups, but without such supportive policies, the economic gap between ethnic groups may further intensify, leading to greater social unrest.          

## Taipei & Tainan & Kaohsiung

Taiwan is the highlight of my trip. This time I plan to visit Taipei and Tainan. The original plan was to fly from Kuala Lumpur to Tainan and then head north. However, it seemed that the only international airport in Taiwan was Taoyuan Airport in Taipei, so we had to fly to Taipei first and then turn back.
![The queue for entering Taiwan is extremely long...](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/AA38F10E-7027-48BC-A157-1F9408232125_1_105_c.jpeg)

![Chiang Kai-Shek Memorial Hall](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/6D026014-163E-4C08-BBEC-9CE59D4AFA17_1_105_c.jpeg)

Taipei feels very similar to Beijing. There are both modern and high-rise buildings, as well as buildings full of Chinese characteristics. Tradition and modernity coexist.  
![Of course, there are as many electric bicycles as in Beijing...](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/B3CA3EEB-BD90-42F1-8628-93089EF8DE62_1_105_c.jpeg)

Taipei was established as a political center at the end of the 19th century. Modern urban infrastructure was completed during the Japanese colonial period. The real large-scale expansion began in 1949 after World War II. So in fact, it can be said that the history is not very long, but the modern & contemporary development is quite telling - the entire city is a museum of modern East Asian history for more than 100 years.   


![Prison for political prisoners during the Kuomintang’s white terror period](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/A9886160-D1B9-4EE6-A642-BC84B3D10462_1_105_c.jpeg)

![Now transformed into an exhibition hall](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20250925195441.png)

Tainan is just the opposite. As the place where Taiwan first formed a city, detailed records can be traced back to the 15th century. It has experienced various periods such as the Dutch rule, the Qing rule, the Japanese rule, etc., and the historical buildings of each period have been preserved. Unlike Taipei, many important neighborhoods and public buildings were renovated into buildings with traditional Chinese elements or modernist styles to highlight the image of the new regime. Due to low development pressure and relatively low land value in Tainan, many Japanese-style, Qing Dynasty or even earlier buildings have survived.

![Tainan 1](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/813283E7-4E12-40EC-A2D5-5DA6F952CD1A_1_105_c.jpeg)

![Tainan 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/67BF249F-7B22-499A-9204-D6517BC5972B_1_105_c.jpeg)


Taiwan has two systems: high-speed rail and MRT. The high-speed rail is quite convenient. It takes about 1 hour and 30 minutes from Taipei to Tainan, and there is a train almost every fifteen minutes. The experience is more like taking the subway, and there are no queues.     

Taxi rides in Taiwan are also quite expensive, almost as expensive as in Vancouver (I later learned that Vancouver is the cheapest place to take a taxi among big cities in developed countries - thanks to the large influx of Indian immigrants), which surprised me. Other prices are similar to those in Beijing, Shanghai and Guangzhou.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/992180BC-BD5D-41E0-B4C5-1F8A55CC5102_1_105_c.jpeg)

In the past few days in Taiwan, I visited a lot of political and historical attractions and museums, and the curation skills were quite good. Almost every exhibition made me enjoy it and it was very profound. ~~Of course, it may also be because the exhibits are actually very limited, so we can only work hard on process design. ~~. Moreover, many museums are open for free, and there are also many staff and elderly people who come to do volunteer work.

It's a pity that the two days in Taipei are weekends, so I can't visit the branches of the Kuomintang and the Democratic Progressive Party (it is said that the Kuomintang welcomes visits).

However, we can indeed feel that due to the changes in cross-strait relations in recent years, the overall narrative is weakening the part of the Republic of China in modern history, and instead emphasizing the process of Taiwan's localization and democratization.    

![National Museum of Taiwan History 1](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/996D02B0-1DA0-4542-8CAE-730559060571_1_105_c.jpeg)

![National Museum of Taiwan History 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/BBF3CC99-A2D3-4BE0-9161-3A1091564328_1_105_c.jpeg)

The National Museum of Taiwan History (located in Tainan) displays a lot of things during the Japanese colonial period, including a lot of photos and objects from that time - such as newspapers, leaflets, daily necessities, etc., which are even far better than museums in Japan. In fact, Japan itself seems to be trying very hard to avoid showing anything related to modern times). There were also many ambivalent attitudes towards Japan among the people at that time.      

![National Human Rights Museum](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/F86083ED-98C8-4A9D-B355-6143D9D2F0CB_1_105_c.jpeg)

![Kaohsiung Museum of History. It only takes fifteen minutes by high-speed rail from Tainan to Kaohsiung](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/5147B187-82AB-4662-AD66-63DB62F7A7B9_1_105_c.jpeg)

I heard that the beauty of the coastlines on the east and west sides of Taiwan is ranked among the top 3-5 in the world. It is very suitable for cycling/driving around the island. Unfortunately, I may not have this opportunity. But I should go to Taiwan again in the future, to the east side and Green Island.    

## Tokyo & Kyoto & Osaka
After leaving Taipei, I met up with friends in Tokyo. I thought there would be fewer people traveling before the May Day holiday in China and the Golden Week in Japan, but there were still a lot of tourists.

![Osaka Expo](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/2EC2B502-75D0-4A68-A1A8-B46D517BC90A_1_105_c.jpeg)

I heard before that many places in the countryside in Japan are actually very touristy, but considering that it was my first time to come to Japan, I basically just visited the urban area and visited some of the more famous attractions-the temples, shrines, etc. I basically took a look at all the popular attractions in these three cities.     

![Osaka World Expo, Pepsi co-branded Coke](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/28B1FBFC-FDDB-4724-A62E-17E4F844BE21_1_105_c.jpeg)

In fact, I personally have only moderate interest in most popular tourist attractions in Japan at this stage. My main sentiment towards Japan is its history after the Meiji Restoration. However, due to the United States' ideological transformation of Japan's de-militarization after World War II, public exhibitions about this period of history are relatively unpopular.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20250809153100.png)

Public transportation in Japan is so developed. There are dense subway lines, but the distances between subway stations are also very long, so you often need to drag your suitcases long distances, and taking a taxi is also very, very expensive. It strengthened my determination to only carry one bag when traveling in the future.  

![Compared to Tokyo and Osaka, there are a lot more political propaganda on the streets in Kyoto. It seems that people in Kyoto are more concerned about politics](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/A9299BF6-3E79-44ED-9A91-013286B23092_1_105_c.jpeg)

In addition, Japan is really like working life in anime and Japanese dramas. There are many office workers on the subway, and everyone wears suits and ties. They range from twenty to fifty or sixty years old.

I have been seeing many North American programmers lamenting and envying life in Japan on social media, which made me very curious. I personally feel that the Japanese way of life and that of North America are almost at the two end points of individualism and collectivism, and the difference is very big. However, if you work for a foreign company in Japan or live in Japan as a foreigner, the experience in all aspects should be quite enjoyable.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/714405E8-5AE6-4098-8857-EC6C83679610_1_105_c.jpeg)


From the streets to scenic spots, you can feel the strong consumerism atmosphere in Japan, which is stronger than many European and American countries. Perhaps it is related to cultural differences. For example, in Europe and the United States, the values ​​of freedom and individualism make some people tend to resist or downplay consumerism; while in Japan, collectivism and social etiquette allow people to integrate into this consumption rhythm more naturally.

On the last day, I walked into Yushukan - a military museum next to a certain shrine with a critical attitude. The exhibition inside really surprised me. Many modern wars have been described as "ordinary conflicts between nations," with aggression and oppression cleverly concealed and even beautified. This narrative is in sharp contrast with the historical facts we are familiar with, and it also makes me feel the existence of revisionism in the real space.

![The end panel of Yushukan claims that "Japan has promoted national liberation and national independence in Asian countries"](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/2961DDF1-6D0C-4EFC-991B-25D950E6269E_4_5005_c.jpeg)


After the war, the United States carried out a thorough transformation of Japan: abolishing militarism, weakening the sanctity of the emperor, and promoting a pacifist constitution, gradually moving Japan towards a modern democracy and peaceful society. Objectively speaking, the transformation led by MacArthur was extremely successful. It not only changed Japan's political system, but also profoundly reshaped social values ​​​​and the education system. West Germany's experience was similar, which led the United States to believe during the Cold War that the "democratization transformation" model could be replicated in other regions.

However, facts show that this transformation cannot be accomplished by simple system transplantation. The United States has tried many times in Latin America, the Middle East, Eastern Europe and other places, but most of them ended in failure or turmoil.

Mainstream education and academia in Japanese society have recognized and accepted responsibility for the war, but spaces like Yushukan still exist, providing a stage for an alternative narrative. A selective memory can be clearly felt in the exhibition hall: emphasizing the sacrifice and suffering of the Japanese, but deliberately avoiding the aggression and oppression of its Asian neighbors. This is not an unintentional mistake, but a deliberate historical reconstruction, packaging "invasion" as "conflict" and disguising "perpetrators" as "victims." In such a narrative, Japan's own trauma is amplified, while the huge harm inflicted on others is almost eliminated.    


![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/E5DC35C6-ABBF-4A5D-8883-C092315A5D7C_1_105_c.jpeg)


This is naturally related to the militaristic education system of modern Japan - the extreme concept of loyalty to the emperor, collectivism, and comprehensive mobilization under the state apparatus - which has shaped the values of unconditional obedience of a generation. In this atmosphere, brutal wars were rationalized, and aggression and atrocities were whitewashed as "justice" and "glory" - values ​​that are completely unimaginable in today's peaceful era, but they became the consensus of the whole society at that time.

The huge trauma it caused was not only deeply imprinted on China, but also extended to the entire East Asia, Southeast Asia, and even Japan itself (modern Asia is really a land full of suffering). To this day, these memories remain an inescapable shadow in regional relations.

For the Japanese right wing, the "invincible" empire that galloped across Asia during World War II still represents glory. It is difficult for them to face the reality of defeat in the war, because a thorough reflection not only means acknowledging the country's past culpability, but also touches the foundation of national identity. Therefore, exhibitions like Yushukan become a kind of psychological compensation - turning failure into another kind of "glory" by rewriting history.
