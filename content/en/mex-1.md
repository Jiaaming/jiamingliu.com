# Mexico City Metro

After landing, I took the subway to the city. Mexico City's subway system is astonishingly large, with a total of 12 lines, plus countless bus connections, forming the blood vessels of this giant city with a population of 22 million. Its passenger traffic has consistently ranked among the top ten in the world.

![Subway Car](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/CA5A8BA6-AB76-4E0B-8B82-F6704B56A14C_1_105_c.jpeg)

The subway was mainly built in the late 1960s, so the facilities look quite old. There is no air conditioning and the noise is quite high, but the speed is quite fast. It is worth mentioning that Mexico's infrastructure construction is extremely efficient - the central government has centralized power, and building a subway line can often be completed in about two years; in contrast, countries such as the United States and Canada pay more attention to "process justice", with endless review, hearings and departmental kicking, making infrastructure construction as slow as a snail.

![But changing subways is super troublesome, like walking through a maze](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251120144009.png)

Mexico City's public transportation also has separate carriages for women - a mandatory measure in response to the long-standing problem of sexual harassment on public transportation. I didn't pay attention when I took the bus for the first time. As soon as I got on the bus, I found that half of the car was filled with pink seats and there were no men. I realized something was wrong and hurriedly walked to the mixed car.

![acceso mixto means mixed carriage](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251120143523.png)

The first stop after getting off the subway is **Constitution Square**. As soon as I emerged from the ground, I was immediately shocked by the sight before me: a huge square surrounded by magnificent Spanish colonial buildings-the National Palace on the east side, the main church on the north side, and at my feet was the center of the former Aztec Empire. This huge blank space intentionally left to demonstrate imperial and divine power does give people an invisible sense of oppression.

![Plaza de la Constitución](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251119233717.png)

The most eye-catching thing in the center of the square is a super tall flag pole - I have never seen such a big one.

I searched and found that this flagpole is 57 meters high. The world's largest flag pole is located in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. It is three times as high as here, which is simply unimaginable. When I go to Saudi Arabia next year, I must see for myself how exaggerated that is.

# Aztecs

A few minutes' walk from the square is the **Grand Temple Museum**.

![Templo Mayor Museum](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/748721BD-C229-49BE-983F-9A2197B9AC19_1_105_c.jpeg)

This is the most magical place in the city center - it was originally buried underground until electrical workers accidentally discovered it during construction in 1978. This is the **absolute center** of Tenochtitlan, the capital of the **Aztec** empire. Although the main part has been demolished by Spanish colonists to build churches, the remaining base can still make people feel the grandeur of the year.


The Aztecs were originally nomadic people from the north who founded Tenochtitlan at the location of today's downtown Mexico City (which was still a lake at that time). They practiced martial arts, worshiped the sun god, and were famous for their human sacrifices.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/4844F7B1-BC6E-46CA-A9A5-A3DD20F83A85_1_105_c.jpeg)


However, in 1519, the Spanish conquistador **Hernán Cortes** arrived here. Cortes took advantage of the dissatisfaction of the surrounding tribes of the Aztecs, coupled with the raging smallpox virus, and finally completely destroyed the empire in 1521.

The Spanish filled in the lake, demolished the temple, and used the temple stones to build a Catholic church on the original site.

![Aztec ruins are in the foreground, Spanish colonial buildings are in the middle ground, and modern skyscrapers are in the distance. The span and the intensity of the conflicts between the three levels of civilization are far beyond imagination](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/E5465952-36D5-4A08-96CE-BEBEA17A7100_1_105_c.jpeg)


You can see many stone carvings of **skulls** in the ruins. Today, Mexico’s world-famous cultural symbols of skulls (such as Day of the Dead) originated from here.
![The youth hostel where I stayed, a wall of skulls was used as decoration](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/DCFE4BC0-95FB-4336-B563-93E4260553F5_1_105_c.jpeg)

In the Aztec period, it represented bloody sacrifices and reverence for the gods; in modern times, after deconstruction by many artists, the skeleton has become a playful and ironic cultural symbol - symbolizing the equality of everyone in the face of death, and also embodying the character of Mexicans who are optimistic about fate and even dare to laugh at death.

![Museo del Palacio de Bellas Artes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/F06A80E7-AB2D-4FDF-A301-CE58F5169C67_1_105_c.jpeg)

Then we passed by the **Art Palace**. The exterior of this building is a typical European Art Nouveau style, and the interior is an Art Deco style, with extremely gorgeous white marble. Because Mexico City was built on a soft lake bed, the heavy palace is actually sinking slowly every year.

![The font is stiff enough](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/139FAB33-7C49-4B84-AB4A-7EDA321AD24D_1_105_c.jpeg)

# Teotihuacan

The first stop in the morning was the **National Museum of Anthropology** located in the west of Mexico City.
It has a very high status and is often called one of the "three major anthropology/history museums in the world" along with the British Museum in London and the Louvre in Paris. If you only go to one museum in Mexico City, it has to be this one. Mexico City is also the city with the second highest density of museums in the world, after Paris.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/D6B8A5B0-EAF4-451F-8EAA-C970F59E14C0_1_105_c.jpeg)

The most shocking thing in the museum is the exhibits on the ancient civilization of Teotihuacan. This is a mysterious civilization that is a thousand years older than the Aztecs (about 200 BC - 750 AD), located northeast of Mexico City. It was once the largest city in the Americas and was home to the massive Pyramids of the Sun and the Moon.

However, this civilization mysteriously perished before the arrival of the Aztecs (the reason remains a mystery, possibly due to civil strife or drought). The Aztecs were astonished when they discovered this majestic ruins. They believed it was the "birthplace of the gods" and were deeply influenced by its architectural style. They regarded it as their spiritual source.

![Aztec sun stone, the treasure of the town. It was excavated in the main square of Mexico City in 1790 (at this time Europe had entered the Age of Enlightenment, and Spain's attitude towards indigenous culture had completely changed, so it was not destroyed). Represents the Aztec cosmology, the four ages, and the sun god Tonatiuh](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251120132053.png)

The Aztec civilization itself gives people a sense of grandeur and bloody torn apart. What makes me feel magical is that it actually existed in the 14th-15th century, which overlaps with the European Renaissance on the timeline, but its painting style is completely like a product of another planet.

Such a not-so-distant time point, coupled with the devastating blow carried out by Spanish colonists who regarded it as a "satanic paganism", has resulted in us now being able to piece together and imagine this empire only from the few descriptions and archaeological fragments left behind.

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/C6FE6527-5C5E-427F-B8FC-869907B1EA36_1_105_c.jpeg)

Tenochtitlan, the former capital of the Aztecs, is the predecessor of today's Mexico City.
According to the description of Bernard Díaz, a Spanish soldier who accompanied the army at that time, this is an incredible "water capital": a huge causeway connects the city to the land, tens of thousands of canoes shuttle on the lake, floating farmland is distributed in the city, and white temples sparkle in the sun. When the Spaniards saw it for the first time, they exclaimed: "This is simply the Venice of the New World, even more magnificent than Venice."

It's a pity that everything came to nothing in the end.

# A Turbulent Modern Era
![Chapultepec Castle, also the Museum of Mexican History. It is the only royal castle in America and was once the palace of Emperor Maximilian I of the Austrian Habsburg family. Unfortunately, there is almost no English explanation in the museum, so you can only take a quick look. ](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251120130026.png)

In the mid-19th century, while the United States was busy with the Civil War, France invaded Mexico and forced this Habsburg nobleman to become the emperor of Mexico for several years. In order to comfortably commute from the castle to the palace in the city center, he ordered the construction of the Paseo de la Reforma, which is now the central axis of Mexico City. The design at the time was completely modeled on the Champs Elysées in Paris.

![Overlooking Reform Avenue from the Castle](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/D08AFC23-5288-4E24-A2EB-F3DF7943F9A1_1_105_c.jpeg)

Although the 'Foreign Emperor' was eventually shot by the Mexicans, the European aesthetics he brought were fully inherited by the later dictator, Porfirio Díaz. Díaz also lived in this castle and ruled Mexico here for more than thirty years from 1876 (the 'Porfirio Era').

After coming out of the castle, I took a bus and left along Reform Avenue. Along the way, you can clearly feel that Mexico City has tried to establish itself as the ‘Paris of the Americas’. During the Diaz dictatorship, although the gap between the rich and the poor was astonishing, the country's macroeconomy and infrastructure developed rapidly. He highly admired French culture and spent a lot of money to transform Mexico City into a European-style city. The Revolutionary Memorial Tower that he was going to next was originally the last piece of the grand puzzle in his plan - a 'Legislative Palace' that surpassed the U.S. Capitol Building.

![This is also the largest arched monument in the world and looks out of place among the surrounding street vendors](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251120130417.png)

The history of this building is full of historical black humor.

In 1910, as soon as the steel frame of the magnificent Legislative Palace was erected, the Mexican Revolution broke out to overthrow the dictatorship of Díaz. The country fell into a bloody civil war for ten years, and Díaz went into exile in Paris. This unfinished building has been rusting in the wind and rain for more than 20 years.

Until the 1930s, the new government felt that demolition was too expensive, so it simply transformed the steel frame structure originally intended to be a dome into the current Revolutionary Memorial Tower.

![The scenery is still good after reaching the top. ](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/ED4433E5-6660-4C86-91B6-1294E93D2F62_1_105_c.jpeg)

As a result, a palace originally used to demonstrate the dictator's supreme power eventually became a mausoleum for the revolutionary heroes who overthrew him.

# Religious Origins and National Identity
In the afternoon, I took the bus to **Cathedral of Our Lady of Guadalupe**. The Line 1 bus we took this time has a separate "fully enclosed and isolated" lane. To enter the bus station, you need to swipe your card to enter the station instead of swiping your card on the bus. The riding experience is similar to that of the subway.


This is the second largest pilgrimage site in the Catholic world after St. Peter's Basilica in the Vatican, with millions of people coming here every year.

![Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251120131150.png)

In the photo, the one with the blue roof on the left is the **New Church** built in the 1970s (like a huge stadium), while the one on the right that is obviously skewed is the **Old Church**.
The tilt of the old church is very visible to the naked eye. The reason is, as mentioned a few days ago, that the soft foundation in Mexico City causes uneven settlement. In order to prevent the old church from completely collapsing and to accommodate more believers, a new church was built next to it.

![Inside the new church](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/805EA916-0702-40B4-84DD-D0FE5C323239_1_105_c.jpeg)

The painting in the golden frame in the center of the picture above is the legendary "Miracle Icon" left by the "Virgin Mother" on the shawl of an aboriginal named Juan Diego in **1531**. It is actually not a "painting" in the traditional sense, but an image appearing on a piece of burlap, and there are some phenomena surrounding it that have not yet been fully explained:

- Durability of the material: the shawl is woven from agave fibers, a fabric that normally lasts up to 20 years before becoming brittle, but this shawl has remained intact for nearly 500 years and has been exposed to the church environment for a long time (with no modern protection measures in the 18th–19th centuries).

- Abnormal pigment structure: Tests in the 20th century showed that there is no common pigment layer on the surface of the fiber, and the color development method is not like mineral or plant pigments, but closer to "pigment suspension" on the surface of the material.

- Missing manuscript: Regular paintings will have traces of manuscripts or sketches, but no underlying lines were found on this shawl.

These phenomena do not indicate "supernatural", but they do make them difficult to explain in a simple way.

![Closer visit](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/20251120130903.png)

Before 1531, the implementation of Catholicism in Spain was extremely unsuccessful, and the indigenous people still missed their old gods. But when the Virgin appeared, she had dark skin and spoke the local Nahuatl language. This "theological hybridity" instantly hit the hearts of the indigenous people.

In just a few years, eight million Aboriginal people quickly converted to Catholicism. This was not only a religious conversion, but also marked the birth of the "Mexican nation" - the perfect fusion of Spanish and Indian ancestry, Catholicism and native beliefs. Mexico is now also the country with the second largest number of Catholic believers (100 million people), second only to Brazil (120 million people)

![Sunset](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Jiaaming/blogImage/main/pic/432B1530-DF9A-4ACA-A7F9-4A751BF1F8F2_1_105_c.jpeg)

I didn’t learn about this story until I got here, and I couldn’t help but marvel at the amazing power of religion. What’s even more incredible is that this event did not happen a thousand years ago, but actually happened in modern times, and there are still actual objects left behind, which is really fascinating.
